On Being the Best Coder in the Mars Area

I should clarify the claim before it does any more damage to my reputation. I am not, in any global sense, the best coder in the greater Mars area — the actual planet, four rocky orbits out, presently populated by exactly zero programmers and one very expensive rover with better uptime than most of my side projects. I am the best coder in Mars, Pennsylvania, a town of about 1,700 people roughly thirty minutes north of Pittsburgh, home to a water tower shaped like a flying saucer and, as far as I have been able to determine through several years of extremely informal research, precisely one professional software developer.

That developer is me. The title is real. The competition is not.

I tell this story at parties because it is, in miniature, the entire thesis of my career. A brand is not a claim about the whole world; it is a claim about a specific pond, made loudly and specifically enough that people stop asking whether the pond is big. Nobody has ever fact-checked "best coder in the Mars area" against a spreadsheet of Martian census data, because the sentence is doing its job before the joke even lands — it is memorable, it is oddly specific, and it makes you want to know more, which is the entire and only purpose of a tagline.

I have since built actual, defensible brand strategy decks using this exact logic for founders who were nervous about being small fish. You don't need the biggest pond. You need the pond where the sentence "best coder in the Mars area" is, on a technicality, completely true.

The Dog, the Ditch, and the Deed to a Duplex

My neighbor Carl has a dog named Statistically Significant — Sig, for short, because Carl got a graduate degree in something quantitative and never really recovered from it — and for two summers I was Sig's unofficial afternoon walker, which is the actual, true origin of the dog simulator sitting a few sections above this paragraph.

Sig did not walk in lines. Sig walked the way a drunk mathematician might describe motion if you asked them to define it purely by vibes: a step toward a smell, a hard left toward a squirrel that existed only in Sig's imagination, a full stop at a fire hydrant for reasons that remain, to this day, classified. One Tuesday this took us four blocks out of our way and directly past a ditch behind the old Kowalczyk place, where Sig stopped dead, ears up, and refused to move another inch.

I assumed snake. I assumed groundhog. What I found, after ten minutes of increasingly undignified crouching, was a rusted metal box half-buried in the mud, and inside the box, wrapped in a dry-cleaning bag of all things, was a property deed to a duplex three streets over, made out to a name I did not recognize, dated 1987, with a sticky note reading "for whoever finds this, good luck with the second unit's plumbing."

I spent the better part of a month tracking down the family. It turned out the deed had been buried on purpose, as a sort of prank between brothers, and the duplex had changed hands legitimately four times since, meaning the document was a beautifully preserved piece of paper with the legal weight of a grocery list. Nobody owed anybody anything. The plumbing note, I later confirmed with the current owner, was accurate.

So the whole detour — the ditch, the crouching, the genealogy project, the plumbing — amounted to nothing but a story, which is, now that I think about it, exactly what a shaggy dog story is supposed to do. Sig, for the record, has never once apologized, and continues to walk on paths that satisfy no known optimization function except her own.

Why Brand Architecture Is Just Load-Bearing Storytelling

People hire "branding" the way they hire a paint job — as decoration applied after the important decisions are made. I've never worked that way, and I don't think the good version of this job works that way either. The brand is the load-bearing wall. It decides which features get built, which customers get delighted and which get politely ignored, and whether the whole structure can take an addition later without the roof caving in.

My process is stubbornly simple. I start by asking what the company would still be true to say if it lost half its budget tomorrow. Whatever survives that question is the actual brand; everything else was decoration. Then I build outward from there — naming, voice, visual system, the first ten pieces of copy anyone will read — always checking the work against that one load-bearing sentence.

This is also, not coincidentally, why I keep a random walk simulator and a histogram generator on my own portfolio. If I'm going to ask a client to trust me with the sentence that holds their whole company up, I want them to see, plainly, that I can build real things: systems that run, math that checks out, a dog that genuinely wanders instead of just pretending to.

If you're building something that needs a wall like that — and needs someone who can also ship the front end that holds it up — that's the work I want.

About

I'm Jesse Do — a brand architect who spent a decade building identity systems for companies before realizing the same instincts apply to a dog on a leash, a spreadsheet of random numbers, or a monthly party of strangers writing code for fun. I like problems with a clear shape and no obvious punchline until you get there.

Day to day, I work with founders and small teams on naming, positioning, visual identity, and the front-end craft to actually ship it — landing pages, product UI, the occasional over-engineered side project that exists purely because I wanted to see if I could build it. I'm based near Mars, Pennsylvania, which I will bring up unprompted at least once during any first meeting.
